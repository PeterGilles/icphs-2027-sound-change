# one label per vowel × gender facet: track midpoint, averaged across age groups
make_vowel_labels <- function(track_data) {
  track_data |>
    mutate(dist_mid = abs(prop_time - 0.5)) |>
    slice_min(dist_mid, n = 1, by = c(Geschlecht, Alter, label)) |>
    summarise(F1_z = mean(F1_z), F2_z = mean(F2_z), .by = c(Geschlecht, label)) |>
    left_join(
      track_data |> summarise(F1_ctr = mean(F1_z), F2_ctr = mean(F2_z), .by = Geschlecht),
      by = "Geschlecht"
    ) |>
    mutate(
      dx = F2_z - F2_ctr,
      dy = F1_z - F1_ctr,
      r = pmax(sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2), 1e-6),
      F2_z = F2_z + 0.25 * dx / r,
      F1_z = F1_z + 0.25 * dy / r
    )
}

1 Sample sizes

Data and measurement. Formants were measured with new-fave (fave-extract 1.2.1, Python 3.10) on the 16 kHz Schnëssen recordings; all settings other than those listed here are new-fave defaults (config: luxembourgish_fasttrack_config.yml, runner scripts in fave_venv/). The target tier is the phone tier, restricted by a regular expression to the Luxembourgish IPA vowels; intervals shorter than 0.05 s are skipped. Formants are tracked with a 25 ms analysis window and a time step of 2 ms (new-fave default: one measurement every 2 ms, so a 100 ms vowel gives 50 raw measurements per formant), with 4 formants and pre-emphasis from 50 Hz. The formant ceiling is optimised per token: 20 candidate ceilings between 4,000 and 7,000 Hz are tried and the best-fitting one is kept (the ceiling chosen per token ranges from 4,000 to 7,000 Hz, mean about 5,500 Hz). For every token, the smoothed F1–F4 tracks are then summarised by a discrete cosine transform and stored as 5 coefficients per formant (parameters 0–4) on log-transformed Hz (*_logparam.csv, the only output requested). The models in this document therefore work on 5 DCT coefficients per formant and token, not on raw measurement points; tracks in the figures are reconstructed from them by the inverse DCT at 100 equally spaced points of proportional time. The phone intervals come from the ZIPA phone tier (a ZIPA phone recogniser fine-tuned for Luxembourgish, with phone timing from MFA forced alignment of the ZIPA phone string; word tier from the corpus transcription), run through new-fave in one-file mode with two local patches that stop it silently skipping output files (fave_venv/LOCAL_PATCHES.md). A single-point measurement heuristic (default 40% of the vowel, 20% for the diphthongs) is also configured but is not used here. After extraction, tokens are filtered to the vowel inventory and to plausible durations (80–400 ms) and Lobanov-2.0 normalised (see below); the table gives what remains.

3 Bayesian functional regression: three-block age (young / middle / old)

Full functional design: mvbind() of all 5 DCT coefficients × 2 formants, Alter3 + Geschlecht + (1 | word) + (1 | speaker_id), shared residual correlation across all 10 response dimensions — with Alter collapsed from the six raw age cohorts into three blocks: young (≤ 24 + 25 bis 34), middle (35 bis 44 + 45 bis 54), old (55 bis 64 + 65+). Additive design only (no Alter3 × Geschlecht interaction — the 65+ cohort is both small, 89 speakers, and skewed 61F/28M, so an interaction would halve effective N exactly where data is already thin).

Fit in brms_dct_age3_batch1.R, brms_dct_age3_batch2.R, brms_dct_age3_aa_downsampled.R, brms_dct_age3_ae.R, and brms_dct_age3_aeu.R. Covers all 12 vowels — ɑʊ, æːɪ, ɑ (batch 1), ɑɪ, əʊ, o, oː, ɜɪ, eː (batch 2), æ (run separately, full sample of 41,595 tokens, fit in 26,557s), æːʊ (run separately, the sparsest vowel: 8,281 tokens, 612 words), and aː (run separately on a stratified 60% downsample, given its own outsized fit time) — ordered smallest-token-count-first within each batch so a run that gets cut off overnight still banks the cheapest vowels. Models cached to brms_models/brms_dct_age3_{diph,mono}_<slug>.rds; posterior draws and difference curves to dct_bayes_draws_age3_batch{1,2}.rds / dct_bayes_diff_age3_batch{1,2}.rds plus dct_bayes_draws_age3_aa.rds / dct_bayes_diff_age3_aa.rds for aː, dct_bayes_draws_age3_ae.rds / dct_bayes_diff_age3_ae.rds for æ, and dct_bayes_draws_age3_aeu.rds / dct_bayes_diff_age3_aeu.rds for æːʊ.

Difference curves and significance colouring. The two difference-curve figures below follow the convention of Riverin-Coutlée et al. (2022, Figs. 10–13): where the difference between two groups is credibly different from zero, the curve is drawn as a solid green line with a green ribbon; where it is not, as a dotted red line with a red ribbon. Here “credibly different” means that the 89% credible interval of the difference, computed from the posterior draws separately at each of the 100 time points, excludes zero. As in their GAMM difference smooths, this is a pointwise criterion: it is not corrected for the many time points along the vowel, so short green stretches should be read with caution.

# summarise a difference table (one row per posterior draw x time point) and mark, per time point,
# whether the credible interval excludes 0; consecutive time points with the same status form a "run"
diff_sig <- function(d, level = 0.89) {
  a <- (1 - level) / 2
  s <- d |>
    summarise(median = median(diff), lo = quantile(diff, a), hi = quantile(diff, 1 - a),
              .by = c(label, formant, contrast, prop_time)) |>
    arrange(label, formant, contrast, prop_time) |>
    mutate(sig = lo > 0 | hi < 0,
           run = cumsum(sig != lag(sig, default = first(sig))),
           .by = c(label, formant, contrast))
  # repeat the first point of every run at the end of the previous run (with that run's status),
  # so that the coloured pieces join without gaps
  bridge <- s |>
    slice_head(n = 1, by = c(label, formant, contrast, run)) |>
    filter(run > 0) |>
    mutate(run = run - 1, sig = !sig)
  bind_rows(s, bridge) |> arrange(label, formant, contrast, run, prop_time)
}

3.2 Rate and acceleration (F1)

Everything above describes where the trajectory is at each point in time (a Lobanov z-score). But two age groups can reach the exact same endpoint by very different routes — one via a brief, fast movement, another via a slow, drawn-out glide — and a position curve alone cannot tell those apart. tidynorm::reframe_with_idct() can also return the first derivative (rate — how fast F1 is changing at each instant) and second derivative (acceleration — how fast that speed itself is changing) of the reconstructed curve, computed analytically from the already-fitted DCT coefficients. No new data, no new model: this reuses the cached brms_dct_age3_* fits, just re-extracted with .rate = TRUE, .accel = TRUE (precomputed and cached to dct_bayes_rate_accel_age3_f1.rds, with æ and æːʊ in dct_bayes_rate_accel_age3_f1_{ae,aeu}.rds from the matching .R scripts; read here, not recomputed).

How to read the three rows below:

  • Position (top row, same as the posterior-draws plot above): where F1 is, at each point in time. Axis reversed, as elsewhere in this document, so close vowel quality is drawn at the top.
  • Rate (middle row): how fast F1 is moving at that instant — the slope of the position curve. Zero means momentarily stationary; the further the curve sits from zero, the faster the articulatory movement at that moment. This row’s axis is not reversed (unlike position), so the sign is read directly: negative means F1 is decreasing (moving toward a closer vowel quality), positive means F1 is increasing (moving toward a more open one) — a dip downward in this row is a fast closing movement. Where the rate curve crosses zero marks a turning point in the position curve (a local peak or trough — often the onset or offset, where the trajectory is momentarily flat).
  • Acceleration (bottom row, also unreversed): how fast that speed itself is changing — not “is it moving” but “is it speeding up or slowing down.” Zero acceleration does not mean the vowel is stationary; it means it is moving at constant speed at that instant. Where acceleration crosses zero marks the point of peak velocity (the trough of the rate curve directly above it) — a direct, mechanical cross-check between the two rows, not an independent measurement.

As throughout: the solid line is the posterior median, the ribbon the 89% credible interval, reconstructed per posterior draw (position, rate, and acceleration are each re-derived separately for every draw, so the ribbons correctly reflect the model’s uncertainty about the shape of the trajectory, not just its endpoints). One caveat specific to derivatives: differentiating any estimated curve tends to amplify noise, so rate and especially acceleration are inherently “wigglier” and relatively less certain than the position curve they’re derived from, even though all three come from the same smooth 5-term DCT fit.

The practical payoff: two groups can differ in how far a glide travels (position), how fast it moves (rate), or how abruptly it starts and stops (acceleration) — three logically separate articulatory questions that a position-only analysis collapses into one. For ɜɪ, for instance, the age difference identified above (old speakers reaching a more close offset) turns out to also be a rate difference: older speakers’ glide reaches a markedly deeper peak velocity than younger speakers’, not just a further endpoint — evidence that the age-related change is not simply “a shorter glide” but a genuinely different articulatory gesture (less ballistic, more gradual, in younger speakers).

4 Distances between vowels across age blocks

The sections above describe how each vowel’s own trajectory changes. A sound change is also a change in the relationships between vowels: a vowel can move toward a neighbour (merger tendency), away from it (contrast enhancement), or move together with it (parallel shift). This section quantifies that from the same cached posterior trajectories (dct_bayes_draws_age3_*.rds; no model is refit), via vowel_distances_age3.R, read here from vowel_distance_summary_age3.rds.

Definition. For two vowels A and B, in one age block and gender, and one posterior draw, the trajectory distance is the mean over proportional time of the Euclidean distance between the two reconstructed F1/F2 points, √(ΔF1² + ΔF2²), in Lobanov-2.0 units. The change in distance with age is Δd = d(young) − d(old): negative means the two vowels are closer in younger speakers, positive means farther apart. Each draw is paired with the same-numbered draw of the other vowel (the vowels come from separate fits, so their posteriors are treated as independent), which yields a full posterior for every Δd.

Limits of what this shows. (i) Δd says that the distance changed, not which vowel moved; read it together with the size of each vowel’s own shift (first table). (ii) The intervals describe uncertainty about the population-mean trajectories only, not the spread between speakers. (iii) Pairs that share a base symbol (for instance ɑ and ɑɪ) are excluded from the plots because their onsets share an articulatory target. (iv) Gender is not tested here: the age3 models are additive (Alter3 + Geschlecht), so any gender difference in Δd would reflect the geometry of the distance, not gender-specific change.

5 Glide shape and amount of spectral change (VISC)

Vowels differ not only in where their trajectories start and end but in how much they move and how they get there. This section treats the two together, as one set of measures computed from the same cached age3 posterior trajectories (dct_bayes_draws_age3_*.rds, already loaded above; no model is refit for the population-level results), on the 10–90% window of the vowel, which leaves out the outer 10% at each end that is most exposed to coarticulation:

  • Amount of movement (how much): the trajectory length TL, the summed length of the path through the F1/F2 plane. This is the measure used for “vowel-inherent spectral change” (VISC) by Jacewicz & Fox (2013) for American English vowels; there it is computed from five measurement points, here from the 80 time points of the reconstructed track inside the window. The extent is the straight onset-to-offset distance (the net displacement). Because the straightness below is path length divided by extent, TL = extent × straightness: TL is the total size, which the other measures split into where the vowel ends up and how it gets there.
  • Shape (how): straightness, and, for glides, the timing measures t50 and lag. Some diphthongs glide fairly directly (ɑɪ), others hold the onset longer and then move late and abruptly, a delayed, convex glide (æːɪ).

Which measures for which vowels. For the six diphthongs (ɑɪ, æːɪ, ɑʊ, æːʊ, ɜɪ, əʊ) all five measures are reported. For the six monophthongs (ɑ, o, oː, eː, aː, æ) only extent and TL. Their movement through the vowel is mostly a drift or a slight offglide rather than a real glide, so the onset-to-offset line is very short; straightness (a ratio to that short line) then explodes and t50 and lag (progress along it) become unstable, which makes those three measures meaningless for them. TL, in contrast, is defined for any vowel.

What the section contains. (1) The definitions and a worked example; (2) the measures per vowel and age block; (3) four pairwise comparisons between diphthongs (æːɪ vs ɑɪ, ɜɪ vs əʊ, ɜɪ vs ɑɪ, ɑɪ vs ɑʊ); (4) how the amount of movement changes with age, including the net direction of the movement; (5) whether that change is a duration effect (token-level brms models with and without log duration) and how the spectral rate (TL per second) changes; (6) the relation between positional change and change in the amount of movement.

Metrics. For every posterior draw, the F1/F2 trajectory (gender averaged, Lobanov-2.0 units, 10–90% window) is treated as a path in the (F2, F1) plane and progress is its projection onto the straight line from onset to offset, scaled to 0–1. Extent and TL are absolute (in Lobanov units); t50, lag and straightness are relative to each vowel’s own glide, so they describe shape rather than size:

  • Extent: onset-to-offset Euclidean distance (the net displacement).
  • TL (trajectory length): the sum of the lengths of all consecutive segments of the path; extent × straightness.
  • t50: proportional time at which progress first reaches 50%. A straight, constant-speed glide gives 0.5; larger values mean the movement is delayed.
  • Lag: mean of (linear progress − actual progress) over the track. Zero for a constant-speed glide, positive for a delayed one.
  • Straightness: path length divided by onset-to-offset distance. 1 = perfectly straight; larger = more curved or detoured.

Picture it. Draw the diphthong as a line in the vowel chart from where it starts (onset) to where it ends (offset). Extent is how long that line is. Now imagine a point moving along it: progress says how far along the line the vowel has got at each moment (0 = still at the onset, 1 = arrived at the offset). A vowel that glides at constant speed has progress 0.5 at the middle of its duration. A vowel that lingers near the onset and then moves late has progress well below 0.5 at the middle, so it reaches 50% only after more than half of its duration (large t50) and is persistently behind the constant-speed reference (large positive lag). Straightness ignores timing and asks only whether the path bends: a vowel that first drops and then moves forward has a longer path than the direct line (value above 1). Because t50, lag and straightness are all relative to the vowel’s own glide, a small and a large diphthong can have the same shape values.

# first proportional time at which `x` reaches `level` (linear interpolation)
first_cross <- function(x, level, t) {
  i <- which(x >= level)[1]
  if (is.na(i)) return(NA_real_)
  if (i == 1) return(t[1])
  t[i - 1] + (level - x[i - 1]) / (x[i] - x[i - 1]) * (t[i] - t[i - 1])
}

shape_metrics <- function(t, F1, F2) {
  P <- cbind(F2, F1)
  chord <- P[length(t), ] - P[1, ]
  ext <- sqrt(sum(chord^2))
  prog <- as.vector(sweep(P, 2, P[1, ]) %*% (chord / ext)) / ext
  path <- sum(sqrt(rowSums(diff(P)^2)))
  tibble(
    extent = ext,
    TL = path,
    t50 = first_cross(prog, 0.5, t),
    lag = mean((t - t[1]) / (t[length(t)] - t[1]) - prog),
    straightness = path / ext
  )
}

Reading the example:

  • Extent is just the length of the straight onset-to-offset line, in the same units as the axes. A and D have identical shape and differ only in size: D’s extent is twice as large while t50, lag and straightness are unchanged. It tells you how far the vowel moves, nothing about how. For the real vowels the unit is the Lobanov-2.0 z-score, i.e. standard deviations of the speaker’s own formant range, so an extent of 1 means the glide spans about one standard deviation of F1/F2 variation. This makes it comparable across speakers and vowels, but it has no direct Hz equivalent.

  • t50 answers “at what fraction of the vowel’s duration has the glide covered half of its distance?” A moves at constant speed and gets there at exactly 0.5 (the middle). B stays near the onset and then rushes to the offset, so it only reaches half-way at about 0.79, i.e. in the last quarter of the vowel. A value below 0.5 would mean the opposite, a fast start followed by a slow approach to the offset.

  • Lag averages the gap between the dashed diagonal (constant speed) and the actual progress curve over the whole duration. A sits on the diagonal (lag 0); B’s curve lies well below it, so its lag is clearly positive (about 0.25). Lag is the more robust version of t50: t50 looks at a single moment, lag at the entire track. Negative lag would mean the vowel is ahead of constant speed.

  • Straightness only looks at the path in the chart and ignores timing. A and B follow the straight line (1.0), even though B is much later. C bulges away from the line and has constant speed along its path, so its straightness is above 1 (here 1.14, i.e. 14% longer than the direct route). Because progress is measured along the straight line, a bulging path also shifts t50 and lag somewhat (C: t50 0.61, lag 0.08 although its speed is constant), so a curved path can look slightly “late” in these two metrics. Check straightness before concluding that a high lag means a delayed movement.

  • TL is the length of the whole path, the number you would get by laying a thread along it. A, B and A’s twin D have TL equal to extent (their paths are straight), D’s is twice as large; C’s TL is its extent times 1.14, longer than the direct line because it bulges. TL is what the table shows when only the amount of movement matters; it is the one measure that is also defined for vowels that barely move.

Summary in one sentence each: extent = how far between onset and offset; TL = how much movement in total; t50 and lag = how late (in time); straightness = how bent (in space).

How to read the table and the first figure. The table lists, for every vowel × age block × metric, the posterior median and the 89% credible interval (the range that, given the model, contains the true population value with 89% probability). The first figure shows the same as distributions: rows are vowels, columns are metrics, and within each panel the three humps are the young, middle and old age blocks. The dot is the median, the thick bar the 89% interval, and the hump the full posterior. Note that each column has its own x-axis, so compare humps within a panel, not across panels. Two kinds of comparison are possible:

  1. Across age blocks within a vowel (humps in the same panel): if young and old humps barely overlap, the glide shape has changed with age; if they overlap almost completely, the shape is stable.
  2. Against the reference values in the table above (0.5 for t50, 0 for lag, 1 for straightness): does the vowel have a delayed or curved glide at all?

How to interpret the results. The comparisons are meant to show that diphthongs which look similar in their endpoints (æːɪ, ɑɪ, ɜɪ all end in a front-high offglide) can still be separated by their timing and path. Differences in t50 and lag therefore indicate a dynamic distinction between vowels; differences in extent indicate a size distinction. Large values in one metric with no difference in the others should be read as one specific property differing, not as “the vowels differ” in general. Always read the metrics together with extent (see limit (v) below).

5.1 Speed through the vowel: how TL relates to the rate section

The section “Rate and acceleration (F1)” above looks at the derivative of the F1 position curve: how fast F1 is changing at each instant, per unit of proportional time. TL is the same idea summed over the whole vowel and over both formants. If speed(t) = √(F1′(t)² + F2′(t)²) is the instantaneous speed of the point moving through the F1/F2 plane, then TL is the integral of speed(t) over the window, and the F1 rate curve is just the F1 component of that speed. The figure below shows the speed profile itself: the segment lengths of the reconstructed 100-point track, divided by the time step, in Lobanov units per unit of proportional time (so one unit means “one full vowel duration”), with the 89% credible interval. The area under each curve is TL; the shape of the curve shows where in the vowel the movement happens (early, late, or evenly spread), and a peak in the middle of a diphthong marks the fastest part of the glide.

To avoid the same word for two different things, the terms are used as follows from here on: speed profile = speed(t) per unit of proportional time (this figure); TL = its integral; mean spectral speed = TL divided by the token’s duration, i.e. Lobanov units per second (the real-time quantity discussed with the duration results below). The F1 rate of the earlier section is the F1 component of the speed profile, in proportional time; it does not depend on duration.

5.3 Is the age change in TL a duration effect?

Older speakers’ tokens are slightly longer for most vowels (median duration by age block in the table below), and a longer vowel gives the articulators more time to travel, so part of the TL difference could be duration. Duration affects TL but not the timing measures (t50, lag), which are in proportional time. To separate the two, TL is computed for every single token (from its own five DCT coefficients, 10–90% window) and modelled per vowel with brms as log(TL) ~ age block + gender + log duration + (1 | word) + (1 | speaker_id) (duration-adjusted) and without the duration term (unadjusted). The age coefficient is a log-ratio; the figure shows it as a percentage change in TL for old vs young speakers (89% credible interval).

How to read it. The duration slope is the elasticity of TL with respect to duration: a slope of 1 would mean TL grows in proportion to duration; the estimates are all below 1 (0.60 for aː to 0.94 for ɑɪ), so longer vowels move more, but less than proportionally. Comparing the two age effects then gives three cases: (i) the effect shrinks but stays credible (the four diphthongs, ɑ, o, eː): about a third to a half of the TL difference is a duration effect, the rest is a genuine difference in the amount of movement; (ii) the effect disappears (oː, aː, æ): the difference was duration; (iii) the adjusted effect turns slightly negative (æːɪ, æːʊ): once duration is accounted for, older speakers’ tokens move a little less.

Population level versus token level. The two analyses answer slightly different questions. The population-level TL is the length of the average trajectory, which smooths out token-to-token variation, so it reflects the systematic shape; the token-level TL is the average of each token’s own path length and therefore also contains token variability. That is why æ has a shorter mean trajectory in older speakers (−0.12) but no credible token-level difference.

The mean spectral speed is TL divided by the token’s duration (Lobanov units per second), the “spectral rate of change” that Jacewicz & Fox mention but warn is confounded with duration, articulation rate and age. Because log rate = log TL − log duration, its age effect is the TL effect minus the duration effect. The rate is modelled directly (log(TL / duration) ~ age block + gender + (1 | word) + (1 | speaker_id)), which gives the age effect on the rate a proper posterior; the figure puts the three effects side by side. A TL difference that comes with a longer duration (so a rate difference near zero) means the old and young speakers move at the same speed, but the old speakers’ vowels last longer; a rate difference means the movement itself is faster or slower.

What the rate results show (old vs young speakers, % change with 89% interval, model-adjusted for gender, speaker and word):

  • Duration is longer in the old block for all twelve vowels (+3% for o and æ up to +10% for æːʊ and oː), so part of the TL difference is expected from duration alone.
  • The four diphthongs with the largest TL increase also move faster, not just further: ɜɪ +18% rate (TL +28%, duration +9%), əʊ +10% (TL +16%), ɑɪ +10% (TL +18%) and ɑʊ +7% (TL +14%). So in these vowels older speakers’ glides are both longer and quicker than the duration alone predicts; the increase in TL is not just a stretching of the same movement. eː, ɑ and o show small positive rate differences (+3 to +4%).
  • For æːɪ, æːʊ, aː and oː the rate is lower in the old block (−3 to −5%) although TL is equal or higher: their TL increase is smaller than their duration increase, so they cover about the same path more slowly. æ shows no credible difference in either TL or rate.
  • How to read the rate against the duration-adjusted TL. Rate = TL / duration forces the elasticity to be exactly 1, whereas the estimated elasticity is lower (0.60–0.94, see above). If movement grows less than proportionally with duration, the rate of the longer old-block tokens is pushed down mechanically. The duration-adjusted TL is therefore the better estimate of the amount of movement net of duration, and the rate is a descriptive real-time quantity; where the two disagree (for example aː, oː) the disagreement is this elasticity effect.

Limits. (i) The intervals describe uncertainty about the population-mean trajectories only, not speaker spread. (ii) The age3 models are additive in age and gender, so shape differences across age blocks arise only through the additive coefficient shifts. (iii) The metrics use the 10–90% window (visc_window in the chunk above), so onset and offset are the points at 10% and 90% of the vowel, not the vowel’s acoustic edges; change visc_window to use another window. (iv) The ɑɪ/æːɪ/ɜɪ split relies partly on the orthographic repair rules, so a shape difference involving these vowels could partly reflect those rules. (v) Progress is measured along the straight onset–offset line, so for vowels with a very short or non-monotone glide (small extent) t50, lag and straightness become less stable; read them together with extent. (vi) TL in the window is partly edge movement: for monophthongs such as æ and aː the speed profile has its two peaks near the edges of the 10–90% window, where the formants are still moving towards or away from the neighbouring consonants, with a quiet plateau in between. Their TL, and its change with age, therefore mixes the vowel’s own movement with the speed of the consonant transitions and any change in the following or preceding contexts; the models contain no context predictors. A narrower window (e.g. 25–75%) would be a sensitivity check. (vii) Token-level TL is not the length of the mean trajectory (see above); the two are reported side by side on purpose and need not agree. (viii) Duration enters the token-level models as the token’s full duration, while TL is taken over the 10–90% window; the models contain speaker and word effects but no other covariates, and the duration slope is estimated across all tokens of a vowel, so it is a pooled slope, not a within-speaker one. (ix) The token-level and rate models are Gaussian on log scale with default priors; their fits are not stored, only the posterior draws of the fixed effects (R-hat at most 1.01 and no divergences in the TL models). (x) Apparent time: differences between age blocks cannot distinguish change from age-grading.

6 Individual speakers and the community change

The sections above describe the community-level age difference: how the average F1/F2 track of an age block differs from that of another block. This section asks how individual speakers sit relative to that difference. A small shift of the average is compatible with a large spread of individuals, and the two views should not be confused: the speaker random intercepts of the models are net of the age block (the block effect has been removed), so they cannot show an age trend by themselves. To put individuals and community change on one scale, each speaker’s full track is rebuilt, in every posterior draw of the age3 fits, from the block’s population track and the speaker’s own deviation (speaker_position_draws_age3.R).

The size of the change differs between vowels. The table gives, for every vowel, the size of the old − young change (gap) and the spread of individual speakers along that change, both in Lobanov-2.0 z. The speaker-position figure below uses the same z scale for every vowel: the young block mean lies at +gap/2 (right) and the old block mean at −gap/2 (left), so the distance between the two dotted lines in a panel is the gap of the table. Vowels with more change therefore have their reference lines further apart, and the width of the speaker curves (the individual spread) can be compared directly across panels.

Gap is computed from the model, not from speakers: the inputs are the average F1(t) and F2(t) tracks of the young (≤ 34) and the old (55+) block, i.e. the posterior medians of the population tracks of the age3 Bayesian fits (women and men averaged within each draw), the same tracks as in the posterior-draw figures. At each of 80 equally spaced time points between 10% and 90% of the vowel, the old − young difference is taken in F1 and in F2 and combined into one distance in the F1/F2 plane, √(ΔF1² + ΔF2²). The gap is the root-mean-square (RMS) of these distances over time, in Lobanov-2.0 z: each distance is squared, the squares are averaged over the 80 time points, and the square root is taken. It is a typical size of the difference between the two blocks over the vowel in which large differences count more than small ones (it is never smaller than the plain mean of the distances), and it is the size that the position scale below is built on, because the position divides by the summed squared distances. (Example ɑʊ: the F1 difference is about 0.30 z early and about 0 from 70% on, F2 differs by at most 0.07 z. At 10%, 30%, 50%, 70% and 90% of the vowel the distances are about 0.31, 0.29, 0.15, 0.02 and 0.07 z; their plain mean is about 0.17 z, their RMS about 0.20 z, which is the gap in the table.) Individual spread is the standard deviation of the speakers’ positions along the change (the position is defined below), taken within the young and old block, pooled, and averaged over the posterior draws (median), then converted to z by multiplying with the gap (spread in gap units × gap).

Construction. Everything comes from the age3 Bayesian fits. In each posterior draw, a speaker’s full track is the population track of the speaker’s block (and gender) plus the speaker’s own deviation, the (1 | speaker_id) random intercepts of that draw, and this track is projected on the old − young gap of the same draw over 10–90% of the vowel. The result is the speaker’s position in gap units: the young block mean is at −0.5, the old block mean at +0.5, and 0 is the midpoint. A community change of one gap unit therefore corresponds to the distance between the two vertical dotted lines in the figure. 500 evenly spaced draws per vowel are used. A single draw of the speaker deviations is not shrunk towards the block average, so the positions of one draw have the full spread of the speakers (with an honest allowance for the uncertainty of each speaker), and every statistic below can be given with an interval over the draws. Two cruder estimates bracket this one: the speakers’ own token means (noisy, so the spread is too large and the separation too small) and the posterior means of the speaker deviations (pulled towards 0, so the spread is too small and the separation too large); their ranges are given in the summary. The figure pools 50 of the draws.

How to read this figure.

  • What goes into a speaker’s position. For each speaker and vowel, and in each posterior draw, the model gives the population track of the speaker’s age block (the average F1(t) and F2(t) of the block for the speaker’s gender, as in the posterior-draw figures) and the speaker’s own deviation from it (the speaker random intercepts: 5 DCT coefficients per formant, turned into a track by the inverse DCT). The speaker’s track is the sum of the two. Block tracks and deviations always come from the same draw.
  • What the position measures. The vowel’s track is a path in the F1/F2 plane. At each time point of the 10–90% window, the young average and the old average are two points in that plane, and the arrow from one to the other is the change at that moment. The midpoint is the track halfway between the two block tracks, (young + old) / 2 at every time point, formant and gender; it is neither the middle age block nor the 50% point of the vowel in time. The position takes the speaker’s track minus the midpoint track, projects it on the young → old arrows, sums over time and both formants, and divides by the summed squared length of the arrows: position = Σ [(speaker − midpoint) · (old − young)] / Σ (old − young)². Moments and formants where the blocks differ most therefore dominate; where they do not differ, nothing is counted. A speaker whose track lies on the midpoint track has position 0, one who follows the young average −0.5, one who follows the old average +0.5.
  • What the dotted lines are. The left line marks where a speaker would sit whose track equals the average track of the young block, the right line where a speaker would sit whose track equals the average track of the old block. The distance between the two lines is therefore the whole old–young difference of that vowel, and every vowel is rescaled so that this distance is exactly one unit.
  • Scale of the figure. The figure is drawn in Lobanov-2.0 z (the position in gap units multiplied by the vowel’s gap), with the sign flipped so that the young block is on the right (+gap/2) and the old block on the left (−gap/2), and the reference lines are further apart for vowels with more change (ɜɪ) than for vowels with less (eː, o); the horizontal spread of the speakers is then directly comparable across vowels. The statistics in the text and tables below stay in gap units, where the block means are at −0.5 and +0.5.
  • Why −0.5 and +0.5 (in gap units). The scale is set up so that 0 is the midpoint between the two block averages and one unit is the full gap. A speaker exactly on the young average is then half a unit to the left of the midpoint (−0.5), a speaker exactly on the old average half a unit to the right (+0.5). If 0 were put at the young average, the lines would be at 0 and 1; centring on the midpoint only makes the picture symmetric. Because the positions come from the model, the two block means lie at −0.5 and +0.5 in every draw by construction (the speaker deviations average out to about zero within a block); the middle block is not fixed by the scale but estimated. (An earlier version used each speaker’s raw token means with the model’s tracks as reference. Those sat at a vowel-specific offset from ±0.5, for example ɑɪ at −0.24 and +0.73, because the model’s block tracks describe an average word type whereas speakers’ token means come mostly from frequent words. Using the model for the speakers too avoids this.)
  • The position is a shadow of the track difference. Take the F1/F2 plane at one moment: Y is the young average point, O the old average point, M the point halfway, S the speaker’s point. S is projected onto the line through Y and O, and the position is where the shadow falls in units of the distance from Y to O (young end −0.5, midpoint 0, old end +0.5). Over the whole vowel the same is done with all time points and both formants together. Distances on the axis are therefore differences along the direction of the change: two speakers one unit apart differ by one full old–young gap in that direction. Differences perpendicular to the change (for example a lower F2 where the blocks differ only in F1) do not show on the axis, so two speakers at the same position can still have different tracks.
  • Draws, not point estimates. A position is computed in each of 500 posterior draws, so every speaker has a distribution of positions and every statistic below has an interval over the draws (89%). A single draw of a speaker’s deviation is not pulled towards the block average (as the posterior mean is), so the spread of the speakers in one draw is the spread to expect in the population; the curves in the figure pool 50 draws.
  • The curves are the speakers. Each curve shows how many speakers of a block (colour) lie at which position. If the young curve peaked at the left line and the old curve at the right line and the curves were narrow, the blocks would be easy to tell apart. The curves are in fact wider than the distance between the lines for most vowels and overlap: that is the individual spread behind a shift of the averages. The middle block lies near the midpoint for most vowels (median block mean between -0.14 and 0.14), closer to the young block for eː (-0.34) and oː (-0.23), and as far towards the old side as the old block itself for aː (0.45) and even beyond it for æːʊ (0.66), so the age pattern is not monotonic in these two vowels.
  • Comparing vowels. The unit of the axis is the old − young gap of that vowel, so the axis is in units of “one community change”, not in z-scores or Hz. This makes the vowels comparable as signal against noise: a vowel with narrow curves (ɜɪ, ɑɪ, ɑ) separates the blocks better than a vowel with wide curves (æːʊ, æːɪ, eː). It does not make them comparable in absolute size, and the differences in width reflect the size of the change more than the spread of the speakers: the gap (RMS over 10–90%) ranges from about 0.10 z (eː) and 0.11 z (æːʊ) to 0.28 z (ɑɪ) and 0.39 z (ɜɪ), whereas the spread of individual speakers is similar across vowels (roughly 0.12–0.22 z). Wide curves therefore mean mainly that the change is small, not that speakers are more variable.

Example: what a position means acoustically, for ɑʊ. A position is a recipe for a track: a speaker at position p has, at every time point, F1 and F2 equal to the midpoint track plus p times the old − young difference (if the speaker deviates exactly along the change). The figure draws these tracks for positions −1, −0.5, 0, +0.5 and +1.

For ɑʊ the young and old block averages differ almost only in F1 during the first half of the vowel: the old block’s F1 is higher by about 0.30 z at 10–30% of the vowel, by 0.15 z at 50%, and not at all from 70% on, while F2 differs by at most 0.07 z (95% of the squared difference lies in F1, 94% before the midpoint of the vowel). A speaker at position −0.5 therefore has an ɑʊ track equal to the young average, with an onset F1 of about 1.44 z, a more close start of the diphthong; a speaker at +0.5 has the old average, with an onset F1 of about 1.74 z, a more open start. Position 0 is an onset F1 of about 1.59 z, −1 about 1.29 z and +1 about 1.89 z. One z-unit is the spread of a speaker’s own vowel means, so 0.3 z is on the order of 5–10% of the F1 frequency (roughly 40–50 Hz for an onset F1 around 700 Hz). Because the later part of the vowel and F2 hardly differ between the blocks, they hardly count for the position: for ɑʊ the position is essentially how open the speaker’s diphthong onset is, and since onset F1 is a noisy measurement with few tokens per speaker, the ɑʊ curves are wide.

Summary. The numbers in this summary are computed in the (hidden) chunk below from the per-draw speaker positions (speaker_position_draws_age3.rds, made by speaker_position_draws_age3.R); they are medians over the 500 posterior draws, with 89% intervals in brackets where given. d is the old − young difference of the mean positions divided by the within-block spread of the positions (in gap units, d = 1 / spread); AUC is the probability that a random old speaker is more old-like than a random young one (0.5 = chance); block share is the share of the between-speaker variance of the positions explained by the age block; overlap is the share of old speakers on the young side of the midpoint (position below 0) and of young speakers on the old side (above 0).

  • A shift of the average with a large overlap. Per vowel, the standardised old − young difference is d = 0.5–2.1 (the 89% intervals are narrow, at most ±0.21); the probability that a random old speaker is more old-like than a random young one (AUC) is 0.66–0.93 (chance = 0.5); the age block explains 7–40% of the between-speaker variance. Depending on the vowel, 14–38% of the old speakers sit on the young side of the midpoint, and 14–39% of the young speakers on the old side. In short, the community change is real and clearly estimated, and for most vowels it is small compared with the differences between individuals.
  • The vowels differ. The separation is strongest for ɜɪ (d 2.1 [2.0–2.2], block share 40%), ɑɪ (1.8 [1.6–1.9], 31%), ɑʊ (1.6 [1.4–1.8], 27%), ɑ (1.5 [1.4–1.6], 25%) and əʊ (1.5 [1.3–1.6], 25%), intermediate for o (1.0 [0.9–1.1]), and weakest for æ, æːɪ, oː, aː, eː and æːʊ (d 0.5–0.9, block share 7–13%). The order follows the size of the gap in the table above, with ɑʊ as the clearest exception: its gap is only medium, but its speakers vary little.
  • The change is not carried by a few extreme speakers. The shift function (old minus young quantile of the positions at each decile, in gap units) is flat: the median over vowels is 0.96–1.02 from the 10% to the 90% quantile, against 1 for the gap, so the whole distribution moves, not just a tail.
  • Cruder estimates bracket these values. With the speakers’ own token means (noisy, spread too large) d is 0.3–1.5, with the posterior means of the speaker deviations (shrunk, spread too small) 0.7–3.0; the per-draw values lie between the two for every vowel.
  • Limits. The positions use the formant tracks over 10–90% only. The within-block spread rests on the model’s assumption that speakers vary around their block with one common, normally distributed spread per vowel: a different spread in the old and the young block, or skewed distributions, cannot show up in the per-draw positions (the token means make no such assumption and are the check). The F1 normalisation caveat from the difference-curve section applies here too.
